## 1. Dynamics of policy-gradient training as selection

Mapping used throughout this section: one training step = one generation; the N answers sampled per step = the
population sample; an escape = the beneficial variant; its probability under the current model p = the variant's
frequency; reward advantage delta (scaled by learning rate eta) = selection coefficient s. Where a formula below is
our own one-line derivation from a paper's equation, it is marked [derived].

### 1.1 Natural policy gradient on a softmax policy is the multiplicative-weights (discrete replicator) update

- Citation: Alekh Agarwal, Sham M. Kakade, Jason D. Lee, Gaurav Mahajan (2021). On the Theory of Policy Gradient
  Methods: Optimality, Approximation, and Distribution Shift. Journal of Machine Learning Research 22(98):1-76.
  arXiv 1908.00261. https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.00261 , https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v22/19-736.html
- Gives us: (a) the exact gradient of the softmax policy, which for one prompt is "probability times advantage";
  (b) the proof that natural policy gradient (gradient preconditioned by the Fisher information) on a softmax policy
  multiplies each action's probability by exp(eta x advantage) and renormalizes, which the authors identify with the
  multiplicative-weights update. This is the discrete replicator equation with fitness exp(eta A).
- Use: the "Idealized model" paragraph and the growth regime of "Predictions for training". Under this update the
  log-odds of an escape against the habitual answer rise by eta x delta per step, exactly, so the takeover time is
  ln((1 - p0) / p0) / (eta x delta) to reach one half [derived]. This is the growth-regime formula in the note. It is exact for a
  tabular softmax (one free logit per answer to a prompt) updated by natural gradient, the setting of Lemma 5.1.
- Key formulas: Section 5, eq. (10): dV/d theta_{s,a} = 1/(1 - gamma) d(s) pi(a|s) A(s,a). Section 5.3, Lemma 5.1
  ("NPG as soft policy iteration"): theta(t+1) = theta(t) + eta/(1 - gamma) A(t) and
  pi(t+1)(a|s) = pi(t)(a|s) exp(eta A(t)(s,a)/(1 - gamma)) / Z_t(s). Quote: the updates "are identical to the classical
  multiplicative weights updates [...] for online linear optimization over the probability simplex". Theorem 5.3:
  V(T) >= V* - log|A|/(eta T) - 1/((1 - gamma)^2 T).
- Confidence: checked in full text (arXiv v5 PDF).

### 1.2 Plain softmax policy gradient: growth is slowed by the rare action's own probability

- Citation: Jincheng Mei, Chenjun Xiao, Csaba Szepesvari, Dale Schuurmans (2020). On the Global Convergence Rates of
  Softmax Policy Gradient Methods. ICML 2020. arXiv 2005.06392. https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.06392
- Gives us: for a one-step problem (a bandit, which is one prompt with whole answers as actions) the gradient of
  expected reward with respect to an action's logit is pi(a) (r(a) - mean reward): the replicator form, but in logit
  space. The convergence bound depends on the smallest probability the best action ever has, as 1/c^2, and a worked
  example shows a bad start costing thousands of steps.
- Use: growth regime and Moderators. Under plain gradient (no preconditioning) a rare escape's logit moves at a rate
  proportional to its own probability p, so dp/dt is about eta delta p^2 and the time to take over scales like
  1/(eta delta p0) rather than ln(1/p0)/(eta delta) [derived from eq. 10]. Which of the two laws a real run follows is
  a property of the optimizer and parameterization, so it belongs with the moderators (learning rate, optimizer,
  normalization). The 1/c^2 dependence is a second, independent statement that small starting probability slows
  plain gradient ascent.
- Key formulas and numbers: Section 3.2.1, eq. (10): d(pi^T r)/d theta(a) = pi(a) (r(a) - pi^T r). Theorem 2, eq. (13):
  (pi* - pi_t)^T r <= 5/(c^2 t), "where c = inf_t pi_t(a*) > 0 is a constant that depends on r and theta_1".
  Section 3.2.1, "Visualization" with Figure 1: r = (1.0, 0.9, 0.1), starting policies (0.05, 0.01, 0.94) versus
  (0.01, 0.05, 0.94); from the bad start "It takes more than 7000 iterations for the algorithm to escape this
  sub-optimal corner!" Theorem 9: lower bound (pi* - pi_t)^T r >= Delta^2/(6 t).
- Confidence: checked in full text (arXiv PDF).

### 1.3 Sampled (on-policy) updates: aggressive updates lock in whatever was sampled

- Citation: Jincheng Mei, Bo Dai, Chenjun Xiao, Csaba Szepesvari, Dale Schuurmans (2021). Understanding the Effect of
  Stochasticity in Policy Optimization. NeurIPS 2021. arXiv 2110.15572. https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.15572
- Gives us: once actions are sampled from the current policy rather than given in expectation, the comparison flips:
  plain softmax policy gradient still converges to the best action (in probability), while natural policy gradient
  with on-policy samples converges to a sub-optimal action with positive probability. The paper defines a
  "committal rate" (how fast an update rule drives an action's probability to 1 if only that action is ever
  sampled) and proves a committal rate of at most 1 on sub-optimal actions is necessary for almost-sure convergence.
- Use: P2 (path) and the waiting regime. The note's P2 picks the escape with the shortest predicted time; this paper
  gives the mechanism by which the first-sampled escape can win even if it is not the best: with fast-committing
  updates, early sampling luck is locked in. It also gives a quantity (committal rate) to state which optimizer
  settings make the path a lottery versus a race decided by delta.
- Key statements: Section 2.2.2, Theorem 3 (stochastic natural policy gradient, Update 5): "(i) with positive
  probability, sum over a != a* of pi_t(a) -> 1"; Lemma 6: the importance-sampled natural-gradient estimate has second
  moment sum_a r(a)^2/pi(a), unbounded as pi(a) -> 0. Section 2.2.1, Theorem 2: stochastic softmax policy gradient
  converges in probability. Section 3, Definition 2 (committal rate, eq. 6) and Theorem 5 (eq. 7: max over
  sub-optimal a of kappa(A, a) <= 1 is necessary). Theorem 6: kappa = infinity for stochastic natural and
  normalized gradient; Theorem 7: kappa = 1 for softmax policy gradient. Intuition quoted after Theorem 6: "each
  sampling of an action a increments its parameter by Theta(1), so if a is sampled t times successively, then we have
  1 - pi_t(a) in O(e^{-c t})".
- Confidence: checked in full text (arXiv PDF).

### 1.4 Rate of mean change = variance (Fisher's fundamental theorem, via the Price equation)

- Citation: Steven A. Frank (2012). Natural selection. IV. The Price equation. Journal of Evolutionary Biology 25(6):1002-1019
  (doi 10.1111/j.1420-9101.2012.02498.x). arXiv 1204.1515. https://arxiv.org/abs/1204.1515
- Gives us: the selection part of change in any mean trait is the covariance between fitness and the trait divided by
  mean fitness; taking the trait to be fitness itself gives Fisher's fundamental theorem, change in mean fitness =
  variance of fitness / mean fitness. Frequencies change as Delta q_i = q_i a_i with a_i = w_i/w_bar - 1 (the discrete
  replicator step). The paper also rewrites the variance as a Fisher information of the frequency change.
- Use: Graded pressures ("the mean penalty falls at a rate proportional to V") and the definition of reachability as
  V. With the multiplicative-weights step of 1.1, fitness of an answer is exp(eta A), so for small eta the one-step
  change in mean reward is about eta x Var(reward) within the prompt [derived]; for a penalty with mean-only advantages, the mean
  penalty falls at eta x V per step at the start; with advantages divided by the group's standard deviation, at
  eta x sqrt(V) [derived; see "What to borrow", item 3]. The covariance form (trait z = indicator of a form, w = fitness) gives the change
  in the frequency of any form, penalized or not, as Cov(fitness, form)/mean fitness: usable for P3-style questions
  about forms that hitch-hike on rewarded answers.
- Key formulas: eq. (2): w_bar Delta z_bar = Cov(w, z) + E(w Delta z). Eq. (7): Delta_S z_bar = Delta q . z =
  Cov(w, z)/w_bar. Eq. (8): Delta_S z_bar = beta_zw Var(w)/w_bar. Section on invariance: Fisher's theorem is "a special
  case of Eq. (5)". Variance identity: Var(w/w_bar) = sum q_i a_i^2 = Delta q_hat . Delta q_hat = F(Delta q_hat).
- Confidence: checked in full text (arXiv PDF).

### 1.5 Waiting regime and sweep time in finite populations

- Citation: Michael M. Desai, Daniel S. Fisher (2007). Beneficial Mutation-Selection Balance and the Effect of
  Linkage on Positive Selection. Genetics 176(3):1759-1798. arXiv q-bio/0612016. https://arxiv.org/abs/q-bio/0612016
- Gives us: the two-regime picture with explicit timescales. A beneficial variant that arises at rate N U per
  generation survives early random loss with probability about c s (c = 2 for Wright-Fisher, 1 in their branching
  model), so new variants become established once every 1/(N U s) generations; once established (at size about 1/s),
  it takes about ln(N s)/s generations to take over. When the sweep is faster than establishment, change is
  establishment-limited ("successional" regime) and proceeds at speed N U s^2; otherwise variants arrive faster than
  they sweep and compete ("concurrent" regime, clonal interference).
- Use: Waiting regime and growth regime of "Predictions for training", and P2. Mapping N = answers per step, U = p0
  (chance a sampled answer escapes), s = per-step log-growth of a sampled escape. The waiting formula in the note
  ("at least 1/(N p0), longer if one sampled escape is not enough") becomes 1/(N p0 x P_establish), with P_establish
  the chance that one sampled escape leads to takeover; the sweep time ln(N s)/s replaces ln(1/p0)/(eta delta) once
  the escape starts from its established size rather than p0. The regime boundary N U << 1/ln(N s) (eq. 1) gives the
  condition under which candidate escapes are adopted one at a time (so "the escape adopted first" in P2 is well
  defined) versus compete (several escapes rise together and interfere).
- Key formulas: section "Heuristic analysis and intuition", subsection "Successional-mutations regime and the
  establishment of mutants": tau_establish = 1/(N U_b s); "the mutant takes of order tau_fix = (1/s) ln[N s]
  generations to fix"; eq. (1): ln[Ns]/s << 1/(N U_b s), i.e. N U_b << 1/ln[N s]; eq. (2): v = N U_b s^2. Section
  "Simplest model": establishment probability "is approx c s with the coefficient c = 2 the Wright-Fisher model and
  c = 1 in ours"; for binary fission "closer to 2.8s". Section "Analysis": fixation time has "a distribution of width
  1/s and mean close to (1/s) ln[N s], rather than the naive (1/s) ln N".
- Confidence: checked in full text (arXiv preprint; the Genetics version was not compared line by line).

### 1.6 Group-normalized policy optimization as a recursion on the probability of success

- Citation: Youssef Mroueh (2025). Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards: GRPO's Effective Loss, Dynamics, and
  Success Amplification. arXiv 2503.06639 (v4, October 2025). https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.06639
- Gives us: closed-form per-prompt recursions for the probability of a good answer (here: of an escape) under group
  relative policy optimization with a yes/no reward, for two normalizations. With division by the group's standard
  deviation, a yes answer gets advantage sqrt((1 - p)/p) and a no answer -sqrt(p/(1 - p)). With a trust region to the
  previous step ("Mirror" variant, the closest to what clipping approximates), the log-odds rise each iteration by
  1/(beta sqrt(p(1 - p) + epsilon)) with standard-deviation normalization, and by a constant 1/beta with mean-only
  normalization. With p = 0 at the start, p stays 0: "GRPO does not create successes and 0 is an absorbing fixed
  point".
- Use: Moderators (group normalization) and the growth regime. Mean-only normalization reproduces the logistic law of
  1.1 (log-odds linear in steps, takeover after about beta ln(1/p0) iterations [derived]). Standard-deviation
  normalization makes the log-odds step grow as p shrinks, roughly 1/(beta sqrt(p)) for small p, so in expectation
  rare escapes grow as fast as common ones and the p0-dependence of time to move sits almost entirely in the waiting
  regime [derived]. In a finite group of G answers with one escape, the normalized advantage of that escape is
  sqrt(G - 1) [derived from eq. 3 with p = 1/G], about 3.9 for G = 16, independent of how rare the escape was. This
  quantifies the note's remark that dividing by the group's spread rescales delta.
- Key formulas: Section 2.1, eq. (3): A = sqrt((1 - p)/p) if r = 1, -sqrt(p/(1 - p)) if r = 0. Section 3, Theorem 2,
  eq. (8): p_n = h(p_{n-1}) (fixed-point iteration, regularization to the reference model). Section 4, Theorem 4,
  eq. (11): logit(p_n) = logit(p_{n-1}) + Omega(p_{n-1})/beta, Omega(p) = 1/sqrt(p(1 - p) + epsilon). Theorem 5 (c): "If
  pref(q) = 0 then pn(q) = 0 for all n". Section 5 (mean-only normalization, Dr. GRPO): Mirror iteration
  logit(p_n) = logit(p_{n-1}) + 1/beta, "an arithmetic progression". Section 6, Practical Takeaways, "Coverage and
  exploration" (quoted above).
- Confidence: checked in full text (arXiv v4 PDF). These are exact-expectation results for the optimal policy of each
  regularized step, not finite-sample stochastic results.

## 2. Reinforcement learning on language models is limited by what the base model samples

### 2.1 Reinforcement learning reweights answers the base model already samples

- Citation: Yang Yue, Zhiqi Chen, Rui Lu, Andrew Zhao, Zhaokai Wang, Yang Yue, Shiji Song, Gao Huang (2025). Does
  Reinforcement Learning Really Incentivize Reasoning Capacity in LLMs Beyond the Base Model? arXiv 2504.13837 (v5).
  https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837
- Gives us: pass@k curves (probability that at least one of k answers is correct) for base models and their
  reinforcement-learning-trained versions: training wins at small k, the base model wins at large k. Answers of the
  trained model have low perplexity under the base model (they sit in the high-likelihood part of the base
  distribution). Coverage at large k shrinks as training continues. An unbiased pass@k estimator, and ablations on
  answers per prompt, KL penalty and temperature.
- Use: waiting regime (training raises answers the starting model already writes); Measurement 1 (the pass@k
  estimator gives the probability of at least one escape among k draws from n >= k sampled answers, the quantity
  1 - (1 - p0)^N that sets the waiting time); P2 (score candidate escapes by their base-model perplexity before
  training); P3 (coverage on other prompts narrows with training); Moderators (answers per step, KL penalty,
  temperature).
- Key numbers and quotes: Section 3.1: "on the Minerva benchmark with a 32B-sized model, the base model outperforms
  the RL-trained model by approximately 9% at k=128". Table 2 (Section 4.1): AIME24 at k = 1024, solved by base only
  13.3%, by trained only 0.0%; MATH500 at k = 128, 3.6% versus 1.0%. Section 4.1: "RLVR mainly sharpens the
  distribution within the base model's prior rather than expanding beyond it." Section 4.4: training-set pass@1
  "improves from 26.1 to 42.5. However, as RLVR training progresses, pass@256 progressively decreases"; raising
  answers per prompt from 8 to 32 improves pass@k "slightly"; KL penalty 0.001 gives "similar pass@1 [...] but with a
  much lower pass@128". Appendix A.2, eq. (2): pass@k = E[1 - C(n - c_i, k)/C(n, k)] with c_i correct among n.
  Appendix C.7: for the few MATH500 problems only the trained model solves, "the base model is able to solve all of
  them when sampling 1024 times."
- Confidence: checked in full text (by the reviewing agent).

### 2.2 Entropy change equals minus a covariance between log-probability and advantage

- Citation: Ganqu Cui, Yuchen Zhang, Jiacheng Chen, Lifan Yuan, Zhi Wang, Yuxin Zuo, Haozhan Li, Yuchen Fan, Huayu Chen,
  Weize Chen, Zhiyuan Liu, Hao Peng, Lei Bai, Wanli Ouyang, Yu Cheng, Bowen Zhou, Ning Ding (2025). The Entropy Mechanism
  of Reinforcement Learning for Reasoning Language Models. arXiv 2505.22617. https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22617
- Gives us: (a) closed-form one-step entropy change of a softmax policy: minus the learning rate times the covariance,
  over the policy's own samples, of log-probability with (probability x advantage) under plain policy gradient, or
  with advantage alone under natural policy gradient; (b) a per-prompt version computed from a group of whole answers,
  usable as a training-time log; (c) an empirical law R = -a exp(H) + b between validation performance R and entropy
  H, fitted on early steps to predict later ones; (d) two controls (Clip-Cov, KL-Cov) that act only on the tokens with
  the largest covariance.
- Use: Moderators (entropy, entropy bonus, temperature) and growth regime. In the note's terms, an escape that has low
  log-probability and positive advantage makes the covariance negative, so entropy rises while it grows; a habitual
  answer with high log-probability and positive advantage drives entropy down. The per-prompt covariance can be logged
  from the same groups that give V and delta, so the entropy trajectory of a run becomes a second readout of whether
  rare escapes or habitual answers are being rewarded. The early-fit-then-predict protocol (36 steps predicting 200)
  is a template for predicting time to move from early data (Tests).
- Key formulas and numbers: Section 3.1, Lemma 1: entropy change approx. -Cov_{a ~ pi}(log pi(a|s), z^{k+1}_{s,a} -
  z^k_{s,a}) (z = logits); Proposition 1: under policy gradient z^{k+1} - z^k = eta pi(a|s) A(s,a). Theorem 1 (plain
  policy gradient, tabular softmax): H(pi^{k+1}|s) - H(pi^k|s) approx. -eta Cov(log pi^k(a|s), pi^k(a|s) A(s,a)).
  Theorem 2 (natural policy gradient, proof Appendix E.4): approx. -eta Cov(log pi^k(a|s), A(s,a)). Section 3.3: in the
  "bandit setting where the prompt x is the state, and whole response y is the action", they "calculate the
  group-wise covariance for each prompt, and average across a batch". Section 2.4, eq. (6): R = -a exp(H) + b; fit on
  "only the first 36 training steps", predicting the next 200 with RMSE "0.9% and 1.2%" (math, code). Section 2.3:
  "73% of the entropy consumption and 76% of the performance gain occurred in just the first 200 gradient steps".
  Section 4.2, eq. (10): per-token Cov(y_i) = (log pi(y_i) - mean log pi) (A(y_i) - mean A). Table 1: the top 0.02% of
  tokens by covariance average 5.654 versus 0.003 over all tokens. Section 4.2 describes adding an entropy term and a
  reference KL term to the loss as "the unsuccessful attempt".
- Confidence: Lemma 1, Proposition 1, Theorems 1-2, eq. (6) and the 36-step fit checked in full text (Theorems 1-2
  re-checked by me in the arXiv HTML). Note: the Section 2 summary box prints the law as R = -a exp(H + b), which does
  not match eq. (6).

### 2.3 A group with equal rewards gives zero gradient; clipping caps one-step growth of rare tokens

- Citation: Qiying Yu, Zheng Zhang, Ruofei Zhu, Yufeng Yuan, Xiaochen Zuo, Yu Yue, et al. (2025). DAPO: An Open-Source
  LLM Reinforcement Learning System at Scale. arXiv 2503.14476. https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14476
- Gives us: the explicit statement that when all answers to a prompt get the same reward, the group advantage and the
  policy gradient are zero, with the filter "keep a prompt only if 0 < number of correct answers < G" (dynamic
  sampling); and a worked bound showing that the upper clip of the probability ratio limits how much a low-probability
  token can rise in one update, fixed by a larger upper clip (clip-higher).
- Use: waiting regime (under a yes/no pressure a prompt contributes signal for the escape only when at least one of its
  G answers escapes, which is the formal content of "nothing moves until escapes are sampled"); Moderators (group
  normalization, eq. 4; the clip range bounds how far one sampled escape can lift its own probability per update,
  which is the note's "longer if one sampled escape is not enough to lift its probability"; dynamic sampling as a
  manipulation that removes zero-signal groups).
- Key quotes and numbers: Section 2.2, eq. (4): A_hat_{i,t} = (r_i - mean({R_i})) / std({R_i}). Section 3.2: "if all
  outputs {o_i} of a particular prompt are correct and receive the same reward, the resulting advantage for this group
  is zero. A zero advantage results in zero policy gradients"; constraint "0 < |{o_i | is_equivalent(a, o_i)}| < G".
  Section 3.1: with epsilon = 0.2, tokens with old probability "0.01 and 0.9" have upper bounds "0.012 and 1.08,
  respectively"; up-clipped tokens have low mean probability, pi < 0.2 (Figure 3a). Section 4.1: epsilon_low = 0.2,
  epsilon_high = 0.28, 16 answers per prompt, prompt batch 512, learning rate 1e-6. Table 1 (AIME24): naive GRPO 30,
  clip-higher 38, dynamic sampling 50 (cumulative).
- Confidence: checked in full text (by the reviewing agent).

### 2.4 Support preservation, its softmax relaxation, and a zero-count bound

- Citation: Fang Wu, Weihao Xuan, Danny Xing, Ximing Lu, Mingjie Liu, Yi Dong, Zaid Harchaoui, Yejin Choi (2025). The Invisible
  Leash: Why RLVR May Not Escape Its Origin. arXiv 2507.14843 (v1, July 2025; v5, October 2026, retitled "... May or May
  Not Escape Its Origin"). https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.14843 , v1 full text https://arxiv.org/html/2507.14843v1
- Gives us (v1): a theorem that on-policy training on verifiable rewards cannot give probability to answers the base
  model gives exactly zero; a relaxation for softmax models where nothing is exactly zero ("empirical support", answers
  with probability above epsilon); a bound on how much probability one update can put on a low-probability answer; the
  closed-form KL-regularized target pi* proportional to q exp(beta R); and a confidence bound after zero observed
  successes. v5 replaces the theorems with per-question counts of support gained and lost.
- Use: waiting regime (the formal limit and its relaxation, which is the regime the note addresses: p0 tiny but
  nonzero); Measurement 1 floor (a stated-confidence version of "cannot resolve p0 below about 1/(number of
  answers)"); P2 (the target pi* proportional to q exp(beta R) states that each candidate's trained probability is its
  starting probability times exp(beta x reward), the same multiplicative form as 1.1).
- Key formulas (v1): Theorem 2.2: "supp(pi_theta(.|x)) subset of supp(q(.|x)). In particular, if q(y*|x) = 0 for some
  correct solution y*, then RLVR cannot discover y*." Corollary 2.3: the trained model's limiting pass@k is at most
  the base model's. "Empirical-Support Relaxation": "the model may assign growing probability mass to completions that
  initially had negligible—but still nonzero—probability", supp_epsilon(q) = {y : q(y|x) > epsilon}, epsilon "e.g.,
  10^-4". Theorem 2.5: under KL(pi_theta || q) <= delta, a low-density answer obeys pi_theta'(y'|x) <= gamma + (1 -
  gamma) e^beta (tau + sqrt(2 delta)). Proposition 2.6: pi*(y|x) proportional to q(y|x) exp(beta R(x, y)). Appendix
  A.7: if not observed in k samples, then with confidence 1 - zeta, pi(y*|x) <= -log(zeta)/k; k = 8096, zeta = 0.05
  gives about 3.70e-4. v5 Section 2.1: "questions with s_p(x) far below 1/n are rarely observed"; v5 Section 3.2:
  "ProRL-1.5B-v2 loses 175 questions while gaining 48".
- Confidence: checked in full text; Theorem 2.2 and Appendix A.7 re-checked by me in v1 HTML. The theorems exist only
  in v1.

## 3. Measuring rare outputs of language models

### 3.1 Importance sampling for probabilities below one over the sampling budget, and how to validate it

- Citation: Gabriel Wu, Jacob Hilton (2024). Estimating the Probabilities of Rare Outputs in Language Models. arXiv
  2410.13211 (v2). https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13211
- Gives us: two families of estimators for output probabilities far below 1/(number of samples): importance sampling
  (independent-token gradient importance sampling; Metropolis-Hastings importance sampling) and activation
  extrapolation; and a validation protocol against brute-force ground truth: targets in a fixed probability band, a
  fixed compute budget, a loss suited to small probabilities, method development on half the input distributions and
  final tests on the held-out half. Their setting is argmax output of small (1-4 layer) models over defined input
  distributions.
- Use: Measurement 3 validation design, copied directly: ground truth by large direct sampling where feasible, score
  with their loss, freeze the estimator before testing on held-out pressures. Section 3.1's reweighting identity is the
  general form of the w-average.
- Key quotes and numbers: Section 2: "if the probability is significantly smaller than 1/n, this sample mean is almost
  always 0". Section 3.1: "If we re-weight our observations properly, this gives an unbiased estimator for the true
  probability", Pr_{x~p}[M(x) = t] = E_{x~q}[p(x)/q(x) 1[M(x) = t]], "but the latter may have less variance". Section 4:
  ground truth from "2^32 random samples", targets "between 10^-9 and 10^-5", budget "2^16 model calls". Section 4.1:
  loss D_IS(p, q) = p/q - ln(p/q) - 1 (Itakura-Saito), "a proper scoring rule" "sensitive to small probabilities".
  Section 2.1: methods developed on 4 of 8 distributions and "finalized before testing on the last four". Section 5:
  "both importance sampling methods outperform Quadratic Logit Decomposition", and "GLD is barely better than outputting
  an optimal constant".
- Confidence: checked in full text (by the reviewing agent).

### 3.2 Masked decoding distorts the distribution; the missing factor is expected future grammaticality

- Citation: Kanghee Park, Jiayu Wang, Taylor Berg-Kirkpatrick, Nadia Polikarpova, Loris D'Antoni (2024). Grammar-Aligned
  Decoding. NeurIPS 2024. arXiv 2405.21047. https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.21047
- Gives us: a proof that decoding with a mask does not sample from the model conditioned on the constraint: the exact
  conditional multiplies each next-token probability by the probability that a continuation from that prefix still
  satisfies the constraint ("expected future grammaticality"), and masking replaces that factor by 1 for every
  allowed token. A worked example of the bias; a trie algorithm (ASAp) that records masked mass along sampled prefixes
  and gives upper bounds on that factor; a KL identity that measures the distortion from model log-probabilities
  alone.
- Use: Measurement 3 (why masked-decoded answers need the weight w and cannot be read as samples of the model's own
  escaping answers; the per-prefix "expected future grammaticality" is the probability that a continuation from that
  spot never writes the form, a prefix-level version of stickiness for Measurement 2 and Definitions). The KL identity

### 3.3 Sequential Monte Carlo with a masking proposal: the weight is the allowed mass, and its mean is unbiased

- Citation: Alexander K. Lew, Tan Zhi-Xuan, Gabriel Grand, Vikash K. Mansinghka (2023). Sequential Monte Carlo Steering
  of Large Language Models using Probabilistic Programs. arXiv 2306.03081 (workshop abstract).
  https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03081
- Gives us: the formal backing for the note's estimator. For a hard constraint, the masked kernel renormalizes the
  softmax over allowed tokens, and the matching potential is the total softmax probability of allowed tokens, which
  "can be seen as an importance weight" equal to the ratio of the original to the masked sampling distribution.
  Algorithm 1 returns an unbiased estimate of the normalizing constant Z, the probability under standard decoding that
  the string satisfies the constraint; resampling (optionally without replacement) keeps it unbiased and can reduce
  variance.
- Use: Measurement 3. With the allowed mass written 1 - m_t, the weight of one masked-decoded answer is the product of
  (1 - m_t), and the average over answers without resampling is the plain importance-sampling estimate of p0 [our
  substitution of their definitions]. Conditions stated by the paper: the mask must equal the indicator of "the set of

  allowed, matching the note's trie that keeps a bare " T"), the target is temperature-1 decoding of the same model,

  of the partition function Z = E_M[prod_t G_t(s_{t-1}, s_t, f_theta)]". Section 3, "Accuracy": SMC "produces unbiased
  estimates of marginal likelihoods", with variance depending on the model; "the proposal [...] should be as close to

### 6.1 Self-reinforcement of repetition

- Citation: Jin Xu, Xiaojiang Liu, Jianhao Yan, Deng Cai, Huayang Li, Jian Li (2022). Learning to Break the Loop:

  predicts escape mass falls at later spots, which the per-spot measurement can check); Tests (their training
  intervention, which reverses the trend, is a candidate manipulation of stickiness).
- Key formulas and quotes: Section 2.1: TP(s^n) = (1/L) sum_l P(x_{n,l} | x_{<n,l}); IP(s^n) = (1/L) sum_l
  1[P(x_{n,l}|.) > P(x_{0,l}|.)]; 16-layer decoder trained on Wikitext-103; 1,000 sentences each from random tokens,
  BookCorpus, Wikitext dev. Section 2.2 (Figure 3): "IP_1 is higher than 90% across the various corpus, meaning that
  even if there is only one sentence-level context repetition, the probability of repetition increases in most
  cases"; "sentences with high initial sentence probability [...] have a stronger self-reinforcement effect". Appendix
  D.1 (Figure 9): five groups by initial probability, higher groups reach higher TP and WR. Appendix B: in generated
  text, a repeated token's probability "increases almost monotonically with the number of historical repetitions".
- Confidence: checked in full text (arXiv HTML). Ceiling values are in figures only.

### 6.2 Structural priming as a contrastive log-probability measurement

- Citation: Arabella Sinclair, Jaap Jumelet, Willem Zuidema, Raquel Fernandez (2022). Structural Persistence in Language
  Models: Priming as a Window into Abstract Language Representations. Transactions of the Association for
  Computational Linguistics. arXiv 2109.14989. https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.14989
- Gives us: the Priming Effect, the log-probability of a target sentence after a prime of the same form minus after a
  prime with the same words in the other form; on GPT-2 large, word overlap between prime and target raises it,
  identical repetition sets a ceiling, it grows monotonically with the number of same-form primes (1 to 5), and grows
  as the prime moves closer to the target.
- Use: Measurement 2 and P3. A content-matched recipe for how much earlier use of a form raises its probability at a
  spot: score the habitual continuation after a context that used the form against a matched context that used the
  alternative. The count and distance designs give the dependence on how many times, and how recently, the form was
  used; measured before and after training it is a carry-over readout.
- Key formulas and numbers: Section 3, eq. [1] / Definition 3.1: PE = log P(T_x | P_x) - log P(T_x | P_y), averaged over
  10 primes per target. Section 6: matched form more probable "60.5% of the time for active, 81.0% for passive, 65.4%
  for prepositional object, and 72.1% for double object". Section 7.1.2: "the presence of lexical overlap greatly
  boosts structural priming effects"; identical prime and target give "a PE of 2.5 for act, 7.2 for pass, 9.2 for po,
  and 10.1 for do". Section 7.3.2 (Figure 4B): "the Priming Effect increases monotonically as the number of congruent
  prime sentences increases". Section 7.3.1 (Figure 4A): proximity "has a highly positive impact on the strength of
  priming".
- Confidence: checked in full text (by the reviewing agent; arXiv HTML). Per-condition values in Figure 3B not read.

## What to borrow, concretely

Items marked [derived] are one-line consequences of the cited equations, worked out for this note; they are not
statements in the papers.

1. Waiting regime (Predictions; P1). Chance that a step of N answers contains at least one escape: 1 - (1 - p0)^N,
   about N p0 when small; the same quantity is pass@N, estimated without bias by Yue et al. eq. (2). Wait to the first
   sampled escape is geometric with mean about 1/(N p0). Wait to an escape that goes on to take over:
   T_wait = 1/(N p0 x P_est), from Desai and Fisher's tau_establish = 1/(N U s) with U = p0 and P_est = c s, c = 2 for
   Wright-Fisher. In training terms P_est is the chance that one sampled escape leads to takeover; it is the quantity
   behind the note's "longer if one sampled escape is not enough". Under a yes/no pressure with group normalization,
   only groups with 0 < escapes < G carry signal (DAPO Section 3.2), and one escape in a group of G gets normalized
   advantage sqrt(G - 1) with the population standard deviation of Mroueh eq. 3 (3.9 at G = 16), or (G - 1)/sqrt(G)
   with the sample standard deviation (3.75 at G = 16), however rare it was [derived]. Floor on p0 when nothing is
   seen: p0 <= -ln(zeta)/k at confidence 1 - zeta (Invisible Leash v1 Appendix A.7); run A's 0 of 153,600 gives
   p0 <= 2.0e-5 at 95% [derived arithmetic].

2. Growth regime (Predictions; P1, P2). Three laws, one per update geometry; which one holds is a measurable property
   of the run:
   - multiplicative weights / natural gradient (Agarwal et al. Lemma 5.1) and mean-only group normalization with a
     trust region (Mroueh Section 5): log-odds of the escape rise linearly, eta x delta (or 1/beta) per step; time to
     one half = ln((1 - p0)/p0) / (eta delta) [derived]. After establishment at size about 1/s, the population-genetics
     version is ln(N s)/s (Desai and Fisher).
   - plain softmax gradient (Mei et al. 2020 eq. 10): logit rate proportional to p, so time about 1/(eta delta p0)
     [derived]; the bound 5/(c^2 t) and the 7,000-step escape from a bad start (Section 3.2.1, Figure 1).
   - standard-deviation group normalization with a trust region (Mroueh Theorem 4): log-odds step 1/(beta sqrt(p(1 -
     p))), so in expectation growth time depends only weakly on p0 and the p0 dependence moves into the waiting time
     [derived].
   Readout: plot the log-odds of escape frequency against step from scored.jsonl and fit linear (first law) versus
   the other two.

3. Graded pressures (Predictions; P1). Price / Fisher: change in mean = Cov(fitness, trait)/mean fitness, and change
   in mean fitness = Var(fitness)/mean fitness (Frank 2012 eqs. 7-8). With fitness exp(eta A): change in mean reward
   per step about eta Cov(r, A) [derived]. With mean-only advantages that is eta V; with standard-deviation-normalized
   advantages A = (r - mean)/sd it is eta sd = eta sqrt(V) [derived]. The same covariance form gives the drift of any
   unpenalized form that co-occurs with rewarded answers (for P3).

4. Estimator for rare escapes (Measurement 3). Weight of a masked-decoded answer w = product of (1 - m_t); mean of w
   is an unbiased estimate of p0 (Lew et al. Section 2.2 and Algorithm 1; Wu and Hilton Section 3.1). Conditions from
   the papers: the mask must equal the indicator of prefixes that can still end without the form (the trie that keeps
   a bare " T" allowed), the weights must use the same temperature softmax as the target, and answers must end.
   Relative variance per answer: E_{p | escape}[w] / p0 - 1 [derived: E_q[w^2] = sum over escaping y of p(y) w(y)]; it
   is small when escaping answers have similar weights. Exact form of "From spots to answers": p0 is the expectation,
   over masked paths, of the product of the per-step escape masses 1 - m_t; the note's e_1 x ... x e_k is that product
   read off one path at its habitual spots [derived]. Validation, copied from the
   papers: (i) on pressures where p0 is large enough to sample (threshold pressures at 4-68% escape), compare the
   w-average with direct sampling, scored with the Itakura-Saito loss and with the estimator frozen before held-out
   pressures are tested (Wu and Hilton Sections 2.1, 4.1); (ii) Park et al.'s identity KL(masked || model) =
   KL(masked || exact conditional) - ln p0 (Section 4), with masked/model = 1/w, gives ln p0 - mean(ln w) =
   KL(masked || exact conditional): the gap between ln(mean w) and mean(ln w) measures how far masked decoding is from
   the model's own escaping answers [derived]; (iii) the ASAp trie gives upper bounds on the
   per-prefix escape probability (Park et al. Theorem 1); (iv) if weights are too spread, resample as in sequential
   Monte Carlo, which keeps the estimate unbiased (Lew et al. Section 3).

5. Spot-level stickiness and correlation between spots (Definitions; Measurement 2). Index the escape mass at a spot
   by how many times the form already appeared in the answer: TP / IP / WR curves (Xu et al. Section 2.1) and the
   contrastive priming effect log P(target | same-form context) - log P(target | other-form context) (Sinclair et al.
   eq. 1). The per-prefix "expected future grammaticality" (Park et al. eq. 1) is the exact probability that the rest
   of the answer never writes the form from that spot.

6. Entropy and covariance as a moderator (Moderators). Per prompt, log Cov(log pi(y|x), A(y, x)) over each group (Cui
   et al. Section 3.3; Theorem 2 for natural gradient, Theorem 1 with pi x A for plain gradient): it is the one-step
   entropy change up to -eta. Clip-Cov and KL-Cov (Section 4.2) are controls that can be varied. The early-fit
   protocol (36 steps predicting 200, Section 2.4) is a template for predicting time to move from early steps.

7. Path: race or lottery (P2). Committal rate (Mei et al. 2021 Definition 2, Theorem 5): with fast-committing updates
   the first-sampled escape can be locked in; with committal rate <= 1 the best one wins. Desai and Fisher's regime
   boundary N U << 1/ln(N s) (eq. 1) states when candidates are adopted one at a time versus compete. Published paths
   to score: Emmons et al. (factorize early, then targeted coefficient errors; deletion tried and never rewarded),
   Skaf et al. (" times " -> "\cdot" -> U+2217; Heads/Tails -> R/L), Kaufmann et al. (arrows, "value becomes 1",
   empty reasoning), DeepSeek-R1 (language mixing).

8. Manipulations of p0 with the pressure fixed (Tests). Prompting a specific code (Skaf Appendix D.1) and a prompt
   that makes one escape likelier (Skaf Appendix D.2); injecting synthetic escapes into 0-10% of each batch (Skaf
   Figure D.1); seeding from a checkpoint where the form is already common (Mathew et al., seeded-blatant start);
   temperature (Yue et al. Section 4.5; the estimator's temperature must match).

9. Carry-over and screens (P3; Scope). Web-frequency ratio and per-100-words rate to pick candidate habitual forms
   (Schoen et al. Table 6, Appendix G); priming effect before and after training (Sinclair et al.); shared
   initialization as the condition for distillation to carry traits, and in-context presentation as the null control
   (Cloud et al. Sections 5.1-5.2); language-consistency reward as a graded per-word pressure (DeepSeek-R1 eq. 7).

## Open questions the literature raises for our predictions

- Which growth law do our runs follow: log-odds linear in steps (Agarwal Lemma 5.1; Mroueh mean-only), a rate that
  scales with p (Mei eq. 10), or near independence from p0 under standard-deviation normalization (Mroueh Theorem 4)?
  With Adam, shared parameters and one gradient step per batch, what is the lift in an escape's log-probability from
  one sampled escape, and does it depend on p?
- What is P_est for a sampled escape in our setting, and can it be read off existing runs (for example the 17 sampled
  escapes in hiding seed 2) by tracking the escape's log-probability at the steps after each sampled escape?
- Is the boundary between the waiting and growth regimes at N p0 = 1, or at N p0 x P_est = 1? Do published runs with
  stated p0 and N (Emmons et al.: 0.5% and 128 per step) fall on the predicted times once learning rate and delta are
  known?
- For graded pressures under standard-deviation normalization, should P1 be stated in sqrt(V) rather than V? Does a
  pair of pressures with equal V but different group normalization separate the two?
- Do spots behave independently (p0 = e_1 x ... x e_k), or does escape mass at later spots depend on earlier spots
  (self-reinforcement, Xu et al.; priming grows with count and proximity, Sinclair et al.)? The masked-path product
  measures the dependent version directly; how far is it from the product of first-spot escape masses?
- How large is the per-answer relative variance E_{p | escape}[w]/p0 - 1 for a word ban with about 10 spots, and is
  sequential Monte Carlo resampling needed to resolve p0 near 1e-57?
- Is the adopted escape the one with the shortest predicted T_i, or the first one sampled? Is the committal rate of
  our optimizer settings above or below 1 (Mei et al. 2021), and does the answer change P2 from a race to a lottery?
- Can the budget model of Kaufmann et al. be written in measured quantities: distance D as -ln p0 of the escaping form
  under the starting model, budget B as steps x learning rate x delta? What would lambda then correspond to?
- Does the per-prompt covariance between log-probability and advantage at step 0 predict which pressures move and how
  fast entropy changes while they do?
- Under distillation, do measured stickiness changes carry only between models with a shared initialization (Cloud et
  al.), and do they carry when the trained model's answers are given in context instead?
- For the unusual words in production reasoning (Schoen et al.), is their escape mass at habitual spots low (sticky),
  or are they only frequent? Which open-weight model shows a measurable analogue?
